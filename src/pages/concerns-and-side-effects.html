In Practice

Concerns and Side Effects

How should you read safety evidence?

Not every study about side effects carries the same weight, and knowing the difference helps you judge the claims on this page:[@kim-side-effects-2025][@jalleh-safety-2026]

  • Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) compare a drug directly against a placebo, so they are the gold standard. However, they are short, leave out higher-risk people, and are too small to catch rare events.
  • Real-world studies follow much larger and more varied groups, but they can only show that a drug and an outcome appear together, not that one caused the other.
  • Reporting databases (FAERS, VigiBase) are good at spotting rare warning signs early, but they tend to over-report problems with new drugs, especially after media attention.

So a worry that shows up only in reporting databases is weaker than one confirmed in trials.

Gastrointestinal effects

The most common side effects by far are nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation. They are dose-dependent and usually strongest in the first weeks, which is why doses start low and rise in steps. In trials of people with type 2 diabetes:[@jalleh-safety-2026]

  • Nausea: 19.3% on a GLP-1 RA vs 6.5% on placebo
  • Vomiting: 7.6% vs 2%
  • Stopped treatment because of side effects: 6.5% vs 3.6%, with nausea the leading reason and people 65 and older more likely to stop

Raising the dose more slowly seems to help. In one trial, increasing semaglutide over 16 weeks instead of the usual 8 led to fewer side effects and better adherence.[@jalleh-safety-2026] Researchers have also pointed out a problem with how these symptoms are measured. Most trials rely on participants reporting symptoms in their own words, with no standard definitions, so one person’s “uncomfortable fullness” may be another person’s “nausea.” This is why some experts are calling for validated GI symptom questionnaires in future trials.[@jalleh-safety-2026]

Gallbladder

Gallbladder problems are the most consistently supported risk. In the LEADER trial, gallstone disease occurred in 3.1% of people taking liraglutide compared with 1.9% on placebo. A meta-analysis of 76 trials found an overall risk ratio of 1.37, and the risk was higher at weight-loss doses and with longer treatment.[@kim-side-effects-2025][@jalleh-safety-2026] Two causes seem to work together:[@kim-side-effects-2025]

  • Rapid weight loss makes bile more concentrated with cholesterol, which encourages stones to form.
  • Slower gallbladder emptying, caused by GLP-1 itself, lets sludge build up.

Thyroid

The thyroid concern started in rodents, where liraglutide caused abnormal growth of thyroid C cells and even tumors. That finding may not carry over to people. Rodent C cells are covered in GLP-1 receptors, while human C cells mostly lack them, and liraglutide did not raise calcitonin in more than 5,000 people screened.[@jalleh-safety-2026] However, most human medullary thyroid cancers do carry the receptor, so the drugs could plausibly encourage the disease in people who are already susceptible.[@jalleh-safety-2026] Human studies are mixed:

  • French insurance data: higher risk of medullary thyroid cancer (HR 1.78)[@jalleh-safety-2026]
  • Scandinavian registry: no link (HR 0.93)[@jalleh-safety-2026]

Because of this uncertainty, anyone with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or the genetic syndrome MEN2 is told not to take these drugs.[@latif-glp1-comparison-2026][@jalleh-safety-2026]

Special populations

Some groups need extra care, or the evidence is still limited:[@jalleh-safety-2026]

  • Pregnancy: not recommended, because animal studies showed harm to fetal growth.
  • Older adults: they stop more often (82.6% of people 75 and older by 24 months vs 68.2% of those under 65), but they still seem to get the cardiovascular benefits.
  • Advanced liver or kidney disease: early observational studies suggest possible benefits, but these need confirmation in trials.

Compounded, counterfeit and “research” products

Some of the most serious risks come not from the approved drugs but from unapproved copies. At the peak in 2024, nearly one in three GLP-1 users got their medication from a compounding pharmacy rather than the manufacturer.[@williams-compounded-glp1-2026] Compounding was temporarily allowed during drug shortages. Once the shortages ended (tirzepatide in December 2024 and semaglutide in February 2025), so did the legal basis for copying. In April 2026, the FDA proposed closing the last large-scale pathway.[@williams-compounded-glp1-2026][@fda-503b-bulks-2026]

As of May 31, 2026, the FDA had received 990 adverse-event reports for compounded semaglutide and more than 730 for compounded tirzepatide, and the real numbers are likely higher.[@fda-unapproved-glp1-2026] Poison control calls about injected weight-loss drugs have risen more than 15-fold since 2019.[@williams-compounded-glp1-2026] The main problems the FDA and clinicians have flagged:

  • Dosing errors: many compounded versions come in multi-dose vials that patients measure themselves, concentrations vary between products, and mistakes have reached 10 to 20 times the intended dose.[@fda-unapproved-glp1-2026][@williams-compounded-glp1-2026]
  • Untested ingredients: salt forms of semaglutide that were never tested in trials, and added vitamins. One 2026 study found tirzepatide and B12 can chemically bond into a new molecule.[@fda-unapproved-glp1-2026][@williams-compounded-glp1-2026]
  • Storage and contamination: products arriving warm, and contamination risk in vials used past 28 days.[@fda-unapproved-glp1-2026]
  • Fakes: counterfeit Ozempic, fraudulent pharmacy labels, and products sold “for research only” that come with dosing instructions for people.[@fda-unapproved-glp1-2026]
